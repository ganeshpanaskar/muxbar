                                                                                                                       
  The Coast Guard had automated the light in 1998. Since then a sensor decided when darkness fell, and a motor turned 
  the great lens without anyone's help. The letter thanked Elias for his service and offered him a cottage in town. He 
  folded it into a neat square and used it to level the kitchen table.                                                
                                                                                                                       
  So he kept climbing. Someone, he reasoned, ought to watch the watcher.                                              
                                                                                                                     
  Most nights there was nothing to see but the slow sweep of the beam across black water. Elias would sit in the lantern
  room with a thermos of tea and a paperback, glancing up whenever the light passed over the rocks below. He liked to
  think the ships could feel him there, the way you can feel someone standing in a doorway behind you.                
                                                                                                                        
  On the night of the November storm, the power failed at 2:14 a.m.                                                    
                                                                                                                        
  The beam stuttered, flickered, and died. Elias heard the motor wind down into silence. Beyond the glass the sea      
  churned in the dark, and somewhere out in it the fishing boat Margaret Ann was coming home late. He had watched her   
  leave that morning.                                                                                                
                                                                                                                      
  The backup generator was supposed to start on its own. It didn't.                                                   
                                                                                                                      
  Elias didn't waste time cursing. He went to the cabinet beneath the stairs, where for twenty-six years he had kept the
  old kerosene lamp oiled and trimmed against exactly this kind of foolishness. His hands shook as he lit it, but the
  flame caught. He carried it up to the lantern room, set it at the focal point of the lens, and began to turn the great
  glass by hand.                                                                                                        
                                                                                                                        
  It was heavy, and his shoulders burned. He counted the turns aloud to keep his rhythm, the way his father had taught  
  him: one for the rocks, one for the harbor, one for the ones still out there.                                      
                                                                                                                        
  At 3:40 he saw the running lights of the Margaret Ann swing wide of the point and slip safely into the harbor.      
                                                                                                                        
  The power came back at dawn. The motor hummed to life again, efficient and indifferent.                             
                                                                                                                
  Elias went home and slept until noon. That evening, at dusk, he climbed the stairs again.
