  Elias didn't waste time cursing. He went to the cabinet beneath the stairs, where for twenty-six years he had kept the
  old kerosene lamp oiled and trimmed against exactly this kind of foolishness. His hands shook as he lit it, but the
  flame caught. He carried it up to the lantern room, set it at the focal point of the lens, and began to turn the great
  glass by hand.                                                                                                     
                                                                                                                      
  It was heavy, and his shoulders burned. He counted the turns aloud to keep his rhythm, the way his father had taught  
  him: one for the rocks, one for the harbor, one for the ones still out there.                                         
                                                                                                                        
  At 3:40 he saw the running lights of the Margaret Ann swing wide of the point and slip safely into the harbor.        
                                                                                                                        
  The power came back at dawn. The motor hummed to life again, efficient and indifferent.                               
                                                                                                                      
  Elias went home and slept until noon. That evening, at dusk, he climbed the stairs again.
